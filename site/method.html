Back to today’s list

This asks one question: if the screen had run on a morning two years ago, and you had sold the put it suggested, would you have been assigned?

It is worth reading because the answer is mildly encouraging and the reasons to distrust it are severe. Both halves matter. What follows puts the limits first, because a number read before its caveat has already done its work.

Four reasons this proves less than it looks like

  1. Only half the model could be rebuilt.

    The screen scores seven things out of 100 points. Rebuilding a past morning recovers 49 of those 100. Option quotes are the problem: the free source serves today’s chain and keeps no history, so there is no record of what a put cost in March 2025. That removes the yield component outright, removes the check that the put would actually fill, and removes half the strike-cushion score. The revenue and margin components were dropped on purpose — using today’s filings to rank a year-ago pick is reading the answer before the exam.

  2. The strike is a guess, not a quote.

    With no historical chain, the test estimates where the strike would have been from how much the stock was actually moving, using Black-Scholes at a zero interest rate. That estimate lands closer to the money than a real strike would have — which means the test assigns more often than the real thing would. The bias runs toward pessimism, which is the safer direction, but it is still a guess.

  3. The universe is today’s list of names.

    Every test date is screened against the stocks that have weekly options now. Anything that was delisted, acquired or fell off the list in between has quietly vanished from the sample. Survivors are easier to look good on.

  4. Two hundred and thirty trades is not many.

    One monthly entry, ten names, twenty-three windows. Across a period in which the market mostly rose. That is enough to say the ranking is not random. It is nowhere near enough to say what it will do next year.

What the test did

On the first of each month, take the price history exactly as it stood that morning — no later bars — run the reduced screen over every name that cleared the safety filters, and take the top ten. Sell a cash-secured put roughly five weeks out, at the strike the estimate puts about 11% below the price. Then look at the next 35 trading days and ask whether the stock closed below the strike.

“Every name that qualified” is all 6,959 trades that cleared the same safety filters — the fair comparison, because it is what picking at random from the eligible list would have given you.

Which is to say: two findings, pulling opposite ways

The screen gets assigned less often than chance. 18% against 22%, and it beat its own pool in 15 of the 23 months. Since not being assigned is the entire point of the exercise, that is the number that matters, and it points the right way. The fuller model also beats the price-only version by a wide margin — 18% against 26% — which says the premium and cushion components are earning their weight rather than decorating it.

But the names it picks go up less than average. +0.7% against +1.8%, and the median is negative. This is not a defect; it is the strategy stated arithmetically. The screen deliberately buys into things that have just fallen. If you are ever assigned, you are holding a stock that was going down for a reason, and the discount you paid may not cover it.

Across all 230 trades the worst single name fell 29%; fifteen fell more than 20%.

What would make this worth more

Historical option quotes, mostly. With a real chain the test could use the strike the screen would genuinely have chosen, score the yield and fill-quality components that are currently missing, and count the premium collected rather than only whether the stock stayed above a line. That is the difference between “the ranking is not random” and “here is what the account would have done.” The data exists; it is not free.

Point-in-time fundamentals would restore the other 25 points. Same answer: it exists, it is not free.

The standing caveat

This is a record of what a piece of software would have selected, not a forecast and not advice. A backtest is the most flattering thing a strategy will ever look like, because it is the only run in which nothing unexpected happens.

Selling puts on stocks that have just fallen means deliberately catching falling knives. High implied volatility is the market pricing a real chance of a large move. The premium is not free, and the numbers above are exactly the record of it not being free.
