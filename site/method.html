Skip to the numbers

How these lists have done

Tested 28–29 August 2026 — four rankings, 42 to 47 monthly entries each, back to September 2022

Back to today’s list

Four lists come off one screen, so each one gets its own test, its own holding period and its own question.

The headline: the put list was assigned on 17% of trades against 22% for picking at random from the same eligible names, and it was assigned less often than that pool in 33 of the 47 months.

Sell puts asks whether the put survived five weeks. Buy asks what the stock did over the same five weeks. Long asks what it did over six months. Buy calls asks what it did over ninety days — and asks it of the shares, because an option cannot be priced backwards. One result is good, two are mixed and one is bad. All four are below as measured — a backtest tuned until it looks good is the one kind worth nothing. One weight on the buy list was set by looking at this test, which is said again beside the number it produced.

A number read before its caveat has already done its work, so every limit below sits in the section it applies to, next to the figure it qualifies. Three apply to all four lists and are stated before any result.

What the test did

On the first of each month, take the price history exactly as it stood that morning — no later bars — run the reduced screen over every name that cleared the safety filters, and take each ranking’s top ten. Then hold. Sell puts and buy are held 35 days; long is held 180; buy calls, 90. Five years of bars give 47 entries at the five-week horizon, 45 at ninety days and 42 at six months.

What the test could not see

  1. Only part of each model could be rebuilt.

    Each ranking scores 100 points. Rebuilding a past morning recovers 49 of them for sell puts, 63 for buy, 50 for long and 58 for buy calls. Two things are missing and neither is free: historical option quotes, and company filings as they stood on the day. The long list is hurt worst. Its whole thesis is a strong chart plus expanding revenue, and revenue is the half the test cannot see — so what follows tests the chart half and calls it the long list.

  2. The universe is today’s list of names.

    Every test date is screened against the stocks that have weekly options now. Anything that was delisted, acquired or fell off the list in between has quietly vanished from the sample. Survivors are easier to look good on.

  3. Forty-seven months is a smaller sample than it sounds.

    470 trades looks like a lot until you notice they are ten names picked on the same morning, which rise and fall together. The real sample is 47 entries, not 470 trades — and the market rose across most of them. That is enough to say a ranking is not random. It is nowhere near enough to say what it will do next year.

Sell puts: did the put survive?

This is the question the list is for. Not being assigned is the point of the exercise, so it leads.

“Every name that qualified” is all 13,422 trades that cleared the same safety filters — the fair comparison, because it is what picking at random from the eligible list would have given you. “Price signals only” drops the premium and cushion components and ranks on the chart alone.

Assigned less often than chance, and by a clear margin

17% against 22%, and it was assigned less often than its own pool in 33 of the 47 months. That is the number that matters and it points the right way. The full model also beats the price-only version by a wide margin — 17% against 25% — which says the premium and cushion components earn their weight rather than decorating it.

The one limit on this number, and which way it runs

The strike is a guess, not a quote. With no historical chain, the test estimates where the strike would have been from how much the stock was actually moving, using Black-Scholes at a zero interest rate. That estimate lands closer to the money than a real strike would have — so the test assigns more often than the real thing would. The bias runs against the model, not for it: 17% is the pessimistic reading, and a real chain would likely have assigned less often still. It is a guess either way, and it is worth replacing with real quotes.

It applies to these numbers only. Buy and long own the stock outright, so there is no contract to estimate.

What changed, and what it does not mean

Until this test the dip — how oversold a name is and whether it has turned — carried 30 of this list’s 100 points, the largest single term in it. At 30 the list came out under its own pool at 35, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 days. At 15 it beats that pool at every horizon in the grid above.

Holding it longer was the other repair, and it did not work. The obvious fix for a list that loses over five weeks is to give it more time. The bottom weight row is that experiment, and it loses in all four columns; two further horizons not shown, 120 and 150 days, lose as well. There was no holding period at which the old weighting started winning, which is what left the weight itself as the thing to move.

15 is the middle of the band that works, not its peak. Every weight from 5 to 20 beat the pool at all four horizons. 5 returned the most at three of them and tied at the fourth, and 15 was taken anyway: a weight read off the top of a curve fitted to 42 mornings is a weight fitted to 42 mornings. There is a second reason. At 5 the buy ten is 79% the same names as the long list; at 15 it is 58%. A list that stops reading the dip stops being a different list.

None of this makes five weeks a long time, and none of it says these are bad companies. Buying a dip outright is a different bet from selling a put against it: the put seller is paid for a stock that merely fails to fall; the buyer needs it to actually rise. And the middle name still goes to the pool — at every horizon in the grid, at every weight in it, the shipped one included. What the green cells have is a few large winners, not a better typical name.

This is the one number on the page chosen by looking at the test

Everything else here was measured against weights fixed before the test existed, which is most of what makes those figures worth reading. The buy weight is not in that position. One term, seven settings, four holding periods, one decision — and the decision was made on the same 42 mornings the grid reports. Re-running a test after picking a setting out of it does not test the setting.

What the run does support is the comparison, which is why the whole grid is printed rather than the winning row: at 30 the list finished last in every column and under the pool in all four, and that was true before anything was chosen. What it cannot tell you is whether 15 will beat 20 over the next 42 mornings. Read the +1.9% above as a description of what happened rather than a forecast — and read it more sceptically than the put figures beside it.

Read the two numbers together, not one of them

+15.0% against the pool’s +10.1% and the index’s +9.2% is the best result on this page. But the middle name returned 3.5% while the pool’s middle name returned 4.8%, so the typical pick did slightly worse and the average is carried by a few very large winners. The honest summary: this ranking finds names that move a long way, and more of that movement was up than down. Holding all ten would have beaten the index. Holding one of them was a coin toss with a wide spread, and half of them spent some of the six months down 20%.

Buy calls: behind the pool, on the shares

The newest list, and the one to read most carefully. It ranks names for buying a long-dated call — but the test cannot buy one, because there is no historical chain to buy it from. So it buys the stock instead and holds it ninety days. Every number in this section is a share return. A call on a name that rose 5% does not return 5%; it returns some multiple of it, and a call on a name that ends below its strike is worth nothing at all. What the test can still answer is whether the ranking picked better names than the pool it picked from — the only question a price history can answer here.

The answer is no, and the option makes it sharper, not softer

+2.2% against the pool’s +5.6% and the index’s +4.8%. The middle name returned +0.4% against +2.6%, and half of these positions ended the ninety days lower than they started. Over this run the ranking picked worse names than taking ten at random from the same qualifying list would have.

Now put the option back on top of that. A call has to cover the time value inside its own price before it makes anything — the list prints that distance on every name, as the move to breakeven — and there is a date by which the move has to happen. On the chains this list was tuned against, time value ran from 44% to 76% of the price, and a stock that ends exactly flat costs the buyer precisely that. 55% of these fell 10% at some point inside the ninety days: on shares that is a drawdown to sit through, on a call with an expiry running it is often the whole position.

The test also sees less of this ranking than of any other. It rebuilds 58 of its 100 points: the ten for the contract itself have no contract to score, the twenty for revenue cannot be read as of a past morning, and half the cheapness reading went with the option history. What is left is mostly the chart.

The falling knives

Two findings, neither the one that was hoped for

Throwing them out would change nothing. Run each ranking again with every knife removed before the top ten is taken, and it drops 0 of the 470 positions — and 0 of the call list’s 450. The best-ranked knife sat at #4,372 of 13,422 on sell puts, #9,908 on buy, #6,229 of 11,780 on long and #7,403 of 12,776 on buy calls. The penalty and the room-to-run rule — which refuses to read a 74% fall as upside — already push them far below anything that reaches a list. A hard gate on top would be a second lock on a locked door.

And over five weeks the knives did slightly better, not worse. +2.0% against +1.3%. They also fell hard more often — a wider spread in both directions, which is what a beaten-down stock is. Over six months they lost the advantage and kept the risk. Held briefly, a knife is a bet; held long, it is just a bad chart.

The rule that catches a name like RBLX is a different one. A knife needs nothing turning, and RBLX had turned — one green stretch inside a 73% fall — so it never met the definition above and the 20 points never applied. Depth on its own now costs 15 points on the put list and 25 on long, charged to any name more than 55% below its high with the 50-day still under the 200-day. On 29 August it cost RBLX 15 points and moved it from first to seventh. The charge is a fixed number, so it cuts deeper when the field is spread out and less when the scores are bunched, which is not tested above.

What would make this worth more

Historical option quotes, for the two lists with a contract in them. For the put list, a real chain would mean the strike the screen would genuinely have chosen, the yield and fill-quality components that are currently missing, and the premium collected counted rather than only whether the stock stayed above a line. For the call list it is the difference between a test and no test at all: with a chain the ninety days could be measured on the contract that was actually bought and sold, which is the only figure anyone buying a call cares about. That is the difference between “the ranking is not random” and “here is what the account would have done.” The data exists; it is not free.

Company filings as they stood on the day, for the buy and long lists. The free source serves about five quarters and nothing older, whatever you ask it for. That is why the long list — the one whose whole argument is revenue growing quarter after quarter — is tested at half its real weight. Same answer: it exists, it is not free.

The standing caveat

This is a record of what a piece of software would have selected, not a forecast and not advice. A backtest is the most flattering thing a strategy will ever look like, because it is the only run in which nothing unexpected happens.

All four lists start from stocks that have just fallen. High implied volatility is the market pricing a real chance of a large move. The premium is not free, the discount is not free, and the numbers above are exactly the record of them not being free.
